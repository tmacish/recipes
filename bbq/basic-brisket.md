# Basic BBQ Brisket

A full packer done straight: heavy black pepper rub, mesquite, tallow and butcher paper through the stall, and a long rest. No injection, no crutch, no sauce. The rub runs pepper-forward in the Central Texas direction, where the salt is there to season and the pepper is there to build bark. Everything that makes this work happens either in the trim or in the rest, and both are the steps people rush.

**Serves:** 12 to 15
**Active:** ~1 hour · **Cook:** 12 to 16 hours · **Rest:** 2 to 4 hours

## Ingredients

### Rub (by parts, 1 part = 1 tbsp makes about 1 cup, enough for a 12 to 14 lb packer)
- 8 parts coarse ground black pepper (16 mesh if you can get it)
- 3 parts Lawry's Seasoned Salt
- 3 parts kosher salt
- 1 part granulated garlic

### Binder
- 1/2 yellow mustard
- 1/2 dill pickle juice

### Spritz
- 50% water
- 50% apple cider

### Cook
- 1 whole packer brisket, 12 to 16 lb untrimmed
- Beef tallow, about 1/2 cup, for the wrap (render it from your own trimmings)
- Pink butcher paper
- Mesquite

## Trimming the Packer

Do this cold. A brisket straight out of the fridge, or 30 minutes in the freezer, is firm enough to cut cleanly. At room temperature the fat smears and you'll gouge the meat. Use a long, sharp, flexible knife.

A packer is two muscles: the **flat** (lean, even, slices) and the **point** (fatty, marbled, burnt ends), separated by a thick seam of hard fat. The goal is a brisket that cooks evenly and sheds nothing that will burn.

1. **Fat cap down first.** Work on the meat side. Shave off the hard, waxy deckle fat and any silverskin. This fat never renders, so anything left here is a bite nobody eats.

2. **Flip fat cap up. Take it to 1/4 inch.** Hold the knife nearly flat and work in long strokes, following the curve. A quarter inch is enough to baste and render; more than that just insulates the meat from smoke and slides off in one sheet at the end.

3. **Open up the seam fat.** Between the point and the flat sits a wedge of hard fat. Take the bulk of it down so the two muscles sit closer to the same thickness, but don't dig through into the seam or the brisket falls apart when you slice it.

4. **Round every edge and square the ends.** Any thin flap, ragged corner, or tapering edge will be charcoal before the center is done. Cut them off. You want a smooth, aerodynamic shape with no points sticking out.

5. **Bevel the thin end of the flat.** The flat tapers away to nothing. Trim that back to where it's a consistent thickness, even if it costs you an inch or two of length.

6. **Knock down the mohawk.** The ridge of fat running along the top of the point renders unevenly and can shield the bark. Take it flush.

Expect to lose 20 to 25 percent of the raw weight. A 14 lb packer comes out around 11.

**Save every scrap of the trimmed fat.** Cube it, render it low in a pan or the smoker for a couple of hours, strain it, and you've got the tallow for step 6 at no cost.

## Method

1. **Season the night before.** Mix the mustard and pickle juice and coat the brisket in a thin film. It's a binder, not a flavor: almost all of it cooks off, and the pickle juice brings just enough acid to keep the mustard from reading as mustard. Apply the rub heavy and even from about 12 inches up so it falls rather than clumps. Get the sides. Rest uncovered in the fridge overnight so the surface dries and the salt works in.

2. **Fire the smoker to 250°F.** Mesquite. Wait for thin blue smoke, never white, and get the fire clean and settled before the meat goes on. See the note on mesquite below, because on a cook this long it matters.

3. **On fat cap up, point toward the heat.** The point has more fat and takes more punishment; the flat is what dries out. Put the thicker end where the heat is heaviest.

4. **Leave it alone for 3 hours.** No spritzing, no peeking. The bark needs to set before you introduce any moisture, and every time the lid comes off you're adding time.

5. **Then spritz every 45 minutes to an hour.** Water and apple cider, half and half. Light passes, just enough to keep the surface from drying and to slow the bark so it doesn't go past mahogany into black.

6. **Wrap at bark set, around 165 to 170°F internal.** Judge by the bark, not the thermometer: it should be deep mahogany and not smudge when you press it. Lay out two long sheets of butcher paper, smear a good layer of tallow down the middle, set the brisket fat side down on it, and wrap tight, folding the ends in like a package. Tallow and paper together push through the stall while still letting the bark breathe, which foil doesn't.

7. **Back on until probe tender, around 203°F.** Start checking at 195°F. The number is a guide, the feel is the answer: a probe should slide into the thickest part of the flat with no resistance at all, like room-temperature butter. If it grabs anywhere, it isn't done. This stretch runs another 4 to 6 hours.

8. **Rest it, and rest it properly.** Vent for 15 minutes on the counter to stop the carryover, then hold wrapped in a cooler or a 150°F oven for 2 to 4 hours. This is not optional and it is not a delay tactic. Slicing early dumps the juice on the board and no amount of good cooking upstream recovers it.

9. **Slice to order.** Separate the point from the flat along the fat seam. Slice the flat against the grain at pencil thickness. Turn the point 90 degrees, since its grain runs the other way, and either slice it thick or cube it and return it to the smoker for burnt ends. Only slice what you're serving; the rest stays in the paper.

## Notes

- **Where it lands.** No chile heat at all. This is pepper-forward, not spicy, and the burn is the sharp front-of-mouth kind from a heavy coarse grind rather than anything that builds. Hot sauce or pickled jalapeños on the side if the table wants it.
- **Mesquite on a long cook.** Mesquite is aggressive and turns acrid over 12 hours, which is exactly why the barbacoa in this repo calls for hickory instead. If you want mesquite here, run it clean: burn it down to coals rather than smoldering splits, and consider mesquite for the first 4 to 5 hours only, then coast on oak or hickory once the meat has taken what smoke it will. Meat stops absorbing smoke around 140°F anyway, so the back half is where bitterness gets in for nothing.
- **Salt.** Lawry's is already salted, so between it and the kosher salt this rub is seasoned at 6 parts salt against 8 pepper. Don't add more on top and don't brine underneath it.
- **The stall is not a problem.** Somewhere around 150 to 165°F the temperature stops climbing for hours while surface moisture evaporates. It's physics, not a stall in your fire. Wrapping is how you shorten it; panicking and raising the pit temperature is how you ruin it.
- **Timing backward.** Figure roughly an hour to an hour and a quarter per trimmed pound at 250°F, then add the rest. For dinner at 6pm with an 11 lb trimmed packer, that's on the smoker by 3 or 4am. Build in a couple of hours of slack, because a brisket that finishes early holds beautifully and one that finishes late ruins the plan.
- **Leftovers.** Slice cold, reheat gently in a covered pan with a splash of stock or the rendered tallow. Freezes well in vacuum bags for 3 months.
