# Kael's Thai Chili Red Enchiladas

Ground beef enchiladas built on a proper toasted-guajillo red sauce, with fresh Thai chilies carrying the burn. The guajillos bring the deep, raisiny body of a classic red enchilada sauce and almost no heat of their own, so the Thai chilies do that job alone and stay easy to dial. It lands at a decent working heat, about where a default batch of Thalen's smoked adobo beef sits, with an obvious route higher for anyone who wants it.

**Serves:** 6 (about 12 enchiladas)

## Ingredients

### Red sauce
- 6 dried guajillo chilies
- 3 large ripe tomatoes
- 1/2 medium yellow onion (roasted and blended smooth; optional, see notes)
- 6 garlic cloves
- 3 fresh Thai chilies, seeded and deveined (this is the heat; do not broil them, see step 2)
- 3 tbsp canola or vegetable oil
- 2 1/2 tsp ground cumin
- 1 tbsp Mexican oregano
- 1 tsp smoked paprika (optional, for extra smoke)
- 1 tsp garlic powder
- 1/4 tsp ground cinnamon
- 1 tsp sugar
- 2 tsp kosher salt
- 1 lime, juiced
- 2 cups beef broth or water

### Filling
- 2 lb ground beef
- 1/2 tsp ground cumin
- 1 tbsp low-sodium soy sauce (background umami)

### Assembly
- 12 corn tortillas
- 1/4 cup canola or vegetable oil, for softening the tortillas
- 2 cups shredded melting cheese (Jack, Colby, or a Mexican blend)
- 1/2 cup fresh cilantro, chopped, to finish
- Crema or sour cream, to finish (optional)

## Method

1. **Toast and soak the guajillos.** Wipe the guajillos clean, tear off the stems, and shake out the seeds. Toast in a dry skillet over medium heat, pressing flat a few seconds per side, just until fragrant and slightly pliable. Do not let them scorch or they turn bitter. Cover with hot water and soak until soft and floppy, about 20 minutes.

2. **Char the fresh vegetables, but not the Thai chilies.** While the guajillos soak, broil the tomatoes, onion (in wedges), and garlic (in their skins) until blistered and blackened in spots and the tomatoes slump. Peel the garlic once cool. **Keep the Thai chilies out of this.** They are small and thin and go black and bitter long before a tomato has even started to soften. Blend them in raw, which keeps their heat bright and clean, or if you want a little char on them, blister them on their own in a dry skillet for under a minute with your eyes on them the whole time.

3. **Blend the sauce.** Drain the guajillos, reserving the soaking water. Blend the guajillos with the charred vegetables, the Thai chilies, cumin, oregano, smoked paprika, garlic powder, cinnamon, sugar, salt, and lime juice. Add soaking water or broth until it pours like a thin gravy. Pass through a sieve for a silkier sauce.

4. **Fry and simmer.** Heat the oil until shimmering and pour in the puree (stand back, it splatters). Frying the raw puree in hot fat is what gives the sauce its depth and rounds the heat. Simmer 12 to 15 minutes, loosening with broth as it thickens. **Taste here.** This is your heat checkpoint. Guajillos add almost none, so the Thai chilies are carrying all of it. It should read as a solid middle burn by now. To climb, blend in another chili, or one with its seeds left in.

5. **Brown the beef.** Brown the beef, breaking it up, until deeply colored. Drain most of the fat. Season with the cumin, salt to taste, and the soy sauce. Stir in a ladle or two of sauce so the filling is glossy and moist. Taste and adjust salt.

6. **Soften the tortillas.** Heat the 1/4 cup of oil in a small skillet until it shimmers. Pass each tortilla through it for a few seconds per side, just until pliable, and stack them under a towel. This is what keeps them from cracking when you roll. Top the pan up as it runs low; twelve tortillas drink more than you expect.

7. **Assemble.** Spread a thin layer of sauce in a baking dish. Fill, roll, and set each tortilla seam-side down. Cover with the remaining sauce (get the ends so they don't dry out) and blanket with cheese.

8. **Bake.** 400°F until the sauce bubbles at the edges and the cheese is melted and just browning, about 20 minutes.

9. **Finish.** Rest a few minutes so the sauce sets. Top with cilantro and a squeeze of lime. Crema on the plate tames any lingering heat.

## Notes

- **Where it lands.** Three seeded Thai chilies sits in the same neighborhood as a default batch of Thalen's smoked adobo beef, and well short of the full-strength version. Seeding matters more than count here: most of the capsaicin lives in the pale veins, not the flesh, so three seeded pods run milder than two left whole. To climb, leave the seeds in one of them. To pull it back, drop to two and lean on the smoked paprika.
- **Onion.** It goes in roasted and blended smooth, where it reads as background sweetness rather than onion. Drop it entirely and lean on the garlic and garlic powder if you'd rather leave it out.
- **Guajillos.** Toast just until fragrant. Scorched guajillo is bitter and unrecoverable. Taste the soaking water before using it; if it's bitter, toss it and blend with broth instead.
- **Color.** Guajillos give a deep brick red, not a bright red. An extra tomato blended in brightens it without adding heat.
- **Fusion accents.** Grated ginger in the beef pushes it further Thai. Leave it out to keep it reading as straight Tex-Mex with a sneaky burn.
