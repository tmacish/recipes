# Tmacish's Bad Ass Chili

Cubed chuck roast instead of ground beef, simmered long enough that the meat gives up and falls apart on its own. The chile base is built from whole dried anchos and guajillos rather than a jar of chili powder, which is the whole difference: toasted and blended fresh, they bring a deep, almost raisiny sweetness that packaged powder lost years ago sitting on a shelf. Heat comes in as a separate layer at the end, so the pot stays approachable and anyone who wants it hotter can build it up at the plate.

**Serves:** 6 to 8
**Prep:** 30 minutes · **Cook:** 2 1/2 hours

## Ingredients

### Chile base
- 3 dried ancho chilies (body and sweetness)
- 3 dried guajillo chilies (brightness and color)
- 1 chipotle in adobo, plus 1 tsp of the sauce (smoke; start here)
- 2 cups beef stock, hot, for soaking

### Chili
- 3 lb chuck roast, trimmed and cut into 3/4-inch cubes
- 2 tbsp canola or vegetable oil
- 1 large yellow onion, diced
- 1 red bell pepper, diced
- 1 jalapeño, seeded and finely chopped
- 4 garlic cloves, minced
- 2 tbsp tomato paste
- 28 oz can fire-roasted diced tomatoes [\*](#appendix-tomato-options)
- 2 cups beef stock, plus more as needed
- 1 tbsp ground cumin
- 1 tsp smoked paprika
- 1 tsp Mexican oregano
- 1 tbsp masa harina (optional, for body)
- 1 tbsp cider vinegar, to finish
- Kosher salt and black pepper, to taste

### Heat accent (adjust to the table)
- 1/2 tsp cayenne, or to taste
- Extra chipotle in adobo, minced

### To finish
- Shredded Colby Jack
- Green onions, sliced thin
- Sour cream or crema
- Lime wedges

## Method

1. **Build the chile base.** Wipe the anchos and guajillos clean, tear off the stems, and shake out the seeds. Toast in a dry skillet over medium heat, pressing flat for a few seconds per side, just until they smell sweet and turn pliable. Do not let them scorch or the whole batch turns bitter. Cover with the hot stock and soak 20 minutes, then blend with the chipotle, its adobo, and enough soaking liquid to make a loose paste. Set aside.

2. **Dry the beef and season it.** Pat the cubes thoroughly dry and season generously with salt and pepper. Wet meat steams instead of browning, and the browning is where most of the flavor in this pot comes from.

3. **Brown in batches.** Heat the oil in a Dutch oven over medium-high until it shimmers. Brown the beef on all sides, working in batches with room between the pieces. Crowding drops the temperature and you get gray meat and no fond. Remove each batch and set aside.

4. **Sweat the vegetables.** Drop the heat to medium. Add the onion, bell pepper, and jalapeño to the same pot and cook until softened, about 5 minutes, scraping up the browned bits as they release. Add the garlic and cook one minute more, until fragrant.

5. **Bloom the paste and spices.** Stir in the tomato paste, cumin, smoked paprika, and oregano, and cook 2 minutes until the paste darkens to brick red and smells toasted rather than raw. This step is quick and easy to skip; don't.

6. **Add the chile base.** Pour in the blended chiles and cook 3 to 4 minutes, stirring, until the mixture thickens and deepens in color. Frying the puree in fat is what rounds the edges off the chilies and pulls the flavors together.

7. **Combine and simmer.** Return the beef and any accumulated juices. Add the tomatoes and stock, and stir to combine. Bring to a boil, then drop to the barest simmer, cover, and cook 2 to 2 1/2 hours, stirring occasionally, until the chuck shreds against the side of a spoon with no resistance.

8. **Taste and set the heat.** Uncover for the last 30 minutes to reduce. **This is your heat checkpoint.** The base is rich but mild by design. Add cayenne or minced chipotle a little at a time, tasting as you go, and stop below where you want it since it climbs as it sits. If it needs body, whisk the masa harina with a splash of stock and stir it in.

9. **Finish.** Off the heat, stir in the cider vinegar and adjust the salt. The vinegar won't read as sour, it just lifts everything and keeps a long-simmered pot from tasting flat. Serve with cheese, green onions, sour cream, and lime.

## Notes

- **Heat control.** All the burn lives in step 8, on purpose. Serve the pot mild and put the cayenne, extra chipotle, and hot sauce on the table so everyone dials in their own bowl.
- **Cubed, not ground.** Chuck cut by hand is what makes this read as a steak chili rather than a taco filling. Don't go leaner than chuck; the connective tissue is what turns silky over a long simmer.
- **Better the next day.** The flavors round out overnight. Cool completely, refrigerate up to 4 days, and reheat gently. Freezes well for 3 months.
- **Thickening.** Reduce it uncovered first; that concentrates flavor instead of just adding starch. Masa harina is the fallback and adds a faint corn sweetness that suits the chile base. A cornstarch slurry works but tastes of nothing.
- **Chile substitutions.** No anchos? Double the guajillos and add a teaspoon of sugar. No dried chilies at all? Three tablespoons of decent chili powder gets you most of the way, but that's the compromise version.
- **Origin.** Adapted from an Outback-style Tasmanian chili, rebuilt from scratch and re-balanced so the heat is a layer rather than baked in.

## Appendix: Tomato Options

The canned fire-roasted tomatoes are the convenient default, not the best version. They're also doing less work here than they would elsewhere, because the chipotle in adobo and the smoked paprika already cover the smoke. Any of these swap in at the same point in step 7.

- **Roast your own.** 2 1/2 to 3 lb Roma or plum tomatoes, halved, cut side up, broiled until blistered and blackened in spots and starting to slump. Chop and add with all their juices. Best flavor by a distance, and the same move the enchilada recipe uses in its step 2, so the two stay consistent.

- **Canned whole peeled, hand-crushed.** One 28 oz can, squeezed apart by hand on the way into the pot. Worth knowing why this beats diced: canned diced tomatoes are treated with calcium chloride so they hold their shape, which is exactly what you don't want in a two-hour braise. They stay stubbornly firm and never melt into the sauce. Whole peeled aren't treated and break down properly. This is a straight upgrade over diced in almost any long-simmered dish.

- **Crushed tomatoes or passata.** One 28 oz can, for a smoother chili with no tomato pieces. Slightly less body, so reduce a few minutes longer at step 8.

- **Fresh, unroasted.** 2 1/2 lb ripe tomatoes, cored and chopped, plus an extra pinch of salt. Only worth doing at peak season. Out of season, canned beats mediocre fresh every time.

- **Adjusting the smoke.** Going to any non-fire-roasted option, push the smoked paprika from 1 tsp to 1 1/2 tsp and you won't miss the char. Going the other way and roasting your own, consider easing the smoked paprika back a little, since real char tastes cleaner than the powdered version.
